Portfolio Plan · Companion to the Build Decision Memo

Three products, three paths —
run as a relay, not a race.

You can absolutely ship all three as separate products. The decision that determines whether this works isn't which products — it's whether they run in parallel or in staggered waves with kill gates. One of those is survivable. The other has a well-documented failure rate.

14 August 2026  ·  Three-track portfolio structure, shared spine, brand architecture, capital plan
Short Answer
Yes — and here's the real argument in favour of it.

Three products means three independent shots at product-market fit instead of one. The merge plan had a single point of failure: if families don't buy memoir books, the whole thesis dies and B and C were never tested. A portfolio hedges that. It also lets each product have its own correct business model instead of forcing A's free mechanic and C's paid artifact into one pricing page. Those are genuine advantages and I'm not going to talk you out of them.

But three products in parallel is the single most reliable way to get zero products.

This is one of the best-documented failure patterns in startups. A census of small US software firms found follow-on products die in R&D at a 45% higher rate than all other project types — and critically, not because they're harder or the team lacks skill. They die because of "extemporary decisions to alter manpower allocations": whichever product has paying customers pulls every engineer toward itself, continuously, and the other products quietly starve.

The failure isn't dramatic. Nobody decides to kill track two. It just never ships, over and over, for eighteen months.

§1 — The Fork

Two ways to run three products

Model 1 · Parallel
Three tracks live at once
→ Needs ~3 dedicated teams, or it degrades instantly
→ Three go-to-markets, three support burdens, three brands to fund
→ Context-switching tax on every founder decision
→ Revenue product cannibalises the others' resources
→ Realistic outcome: one mediocre product, two abandoned
Model 2 · Relay
Staggered launches, shared spine, kill gates
→ One track in Build, one in Grow, one in Idle — never two builds
→ Each launch reuses ~60% of the previous track's code
→ Track N's revenue funds Track N+1
→ Explicit gate: a track only starts when the prior one hits its number
→ Realistic outcome: 3 products live by month 30
The rule that makes this work

Never more than one product in active build at a time. A product in market is allowed; a product in construction is not.

This is the whole discipline. It costs you nothing in ambition — you still end up with three products — and it removes the exact mechanism that kills portfolios. Write it down, and treat any exception as a formal decision with a written rationale, not a busy week.

§2 — Order

Which goes first, and why that order

The sequencing is not arbitrary. Each track must hand the next one something it couldn't buy: cash, users, or infrastructure.

Wave Product Why here What it hands the next wave
1 Grin Legacy
Product C, cut down		 Only one that bills a customer with zero network. Funds everything else. Cash + the shared spine (auth, media pipeline, transcription, billing, storage) + families already inviting relatives.
2 GrinSocial
Product B, standalone		 Needs seeded density to not feel empty. Wave 1 supplies the seed population. The matching engine + group system + moderation tooling — which is roughly 80% of Product A's backend.
3 GrinLuck
Product A, isolated entity		 Highest legal exposure, zero proven revenue. Goes last, when you can fund trust & safety properly. Nothing — and that's deliberate. Nothing downstream may depend on A, so A can be killed at any moment without collateral damage.

Why A moves from wave 3 rather than wave 1, even though it's the easiest to build: buildability is the least important variable here. A is a weekend of engineering and a decade of liability. Building it first means carrying A.M. v. Omegle-shaped risk and UK Online Safety Act age-assurance costs (penalties to £18M or 10% of global turnover) before you have a single rupee of revenue to defend yourself with. Easy to build ≠ cheap to own.

§4 — Shared Spine

What makes three products affordable

Three products does not have to mean three codebases. The one caveat in the "don't build two products" literature is telling: micro-apps are fine as long as they share the same code base. So the portfolio only works if you build the spine once, deliberately, in Wave 1.

Shared service Legacy Social Luck Built in
Identity & auth✓✓✓Wave 1
Messaging / realtime✓✓✓Wave 1
Media pipeline (voice, transcode)✓✓—Wave 1
Transcription + LLM shaping✓——Wave 1
Billing & subscriptions✓✓✓Wave 1
Consent / permanence engine✓✓✓Wave 1
Groups & membership lifecycle✓✓—Wave 2
Matching engine (tag → random)—✓✓Wave 2
Trust & safety / moderation✓✓✓Wave 2, hardened in 3
Age assurance—✓✓Wave 2

Read the last column carefully. By the time you build GrinLuck in Wave 3, the matching engine, messaging, consent logic, moderation tooling and age assurance all already exist and are battle-tested by real users. Wave 3 becomes maybe eight weeks of work instead of six months. That is why the order matters more than the count.

§5 — Brand Architecture

Hybrid: endorse two, quarantine one

Three products forces a branding decision you could dodge in the merge plan. Pure branded-house is cheapest and best for SEO and trust transfer — but it concentrates reputational risk, and one of your products is a reputational hazard. Pure house-of-brands isolates risk but triples marketing cost, which you cannot afford. So: hybrid.

Endorsed — shared equity
Grin Legacy & Grin Social

Same parent name, shared design system, one domain with subpaths, one SEO footprint, one support brand. Trust earned by Legacy flows straight into Social — and both are trust-positive categories, so the flow is safe in both directions.

grin.com/legacy · grin.com/social
Quarantined — zero shared equity
Serendipity (not "GrinLuck")

Separate legal entity, separate domain, separate company page, no shared founder bios, no cross-links, no "a Grin company" footer. Reuses the private codebase; shares nothing public-facing.

serendipity.app — unaffiliated in public

This is the one place I'd hold firm. The name "GrinLuck" is the problem — it does exactly what risk isolation must prevent, by advertising the family connection in the product name itself. Drop the Grin prefix on Product A entirely. Risk isolation is the entire reason a house-of-brands structure exists; a shared prefix throws it away for free.

§6 — Gates

The gates are the strategy

Without these, "three products in sequence" quietly becomes "three products in parallel" by month nine. Decide the numbers now, while you're calm and nothing is at stake.

Gate 1 · Month 12 — may we start building GrinSocial?
All four must be true:
① 250+ paying Legacy customers  ·  ② gross margin ≥ 50% after print and fulfilment  ·  ③ ≥60% of storytellers complete 20+ stories  ·  ④ Legacy runs on ≤1 engineer's ongoing attention
If not met: do not start Wave 2. Spend another 6 months on Legacy or kill the company. ④ is the one founders fudge — if Legacy still eats the whole team, you do not have a product, you have a job.
Gate 2 · Month 24 — may we start building Serendipity?
All five must be true:
① Legacy profitable and self-running  ·  ② GrinSocial D30 retention ≥ 25%  ·  ③ moderation tooling handling real abuse reports at acceptable cost  ·  ④ budget for a dedicated trust & safety hire  ·  ⑤ written legal opinion on age assurance in every launch market
If not met: skip Wave 3 permanently. Nothing depends on it. A two-product company that's profitable beats a three-product company in litigation.
The anti-drift rule

Any track that fails its gate twice in a row gets killed, not paused. Paused products are worse than dead ones: they consume planning attention, roadmap space and founder guilt while producing nothing. Write the kill decision down with a date.
